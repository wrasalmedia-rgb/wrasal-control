Opening Situation
Alex is a direct support professional who works across settings in one week:

- At an after-school program, 9-year-old Devon says his 16-year-old brother watches him alone until midnight most nights while their parent works, and “we sometimes don’t have food — I’m really hungry.” Alex notices Devon’s clothes are unwashed and he missed school twice.
- At a home visit with a partner agency, 79-year-old Ms. Ortiz, who lives alone with early dementia, tells Alex her new “friend” offered to “manage bills for free” and now her checks are late and her bank calls about unusual withdrawals. Ms. Ortiz is unsure what happened and does not want to upset this person.
- At a supported residence for adults with intellectual disabilities, Alex overhears a coworker say, “He’s nonverbal and won’t remember — just leave him in soiled briefs until shift change, and borrow his gift card for lunch — he won’t notice.”

Alex feels the three stories overlap but are not the same — is this neglect, exploitation, or inappropriate conduct? Where do the lines sit across child, older adult, and disability settings? How does this chapter relate to **M04 Child Abuse** and **M05 Elder Abuse** without pasting the same reporting numbers twice? And how should consent, capacity, and possible protective steps be explained without pretending one rule fits everyone?

Distinguish financial exploitation from legitimate help — and distinguish personal relationships from paid caregiving boundaries.
Identify boundary and conduct indicators (power imbalance, secrecy, gifts, favoritism, isolation, misuse of access) that call for pause and consultation.

1. Why Distinguish These Three — Same Pattern Can Mean Different Things by Person and Setting
Words shape response. “Neglect,” “exploitation,” and “inappropriate conduct” often appear together, but laws, agency policies, and rights differ by **who the person is and where you are**:

- A hungry child left alone at night points toward child welfare definitions and reporting described in **M04 Chapter 6 (SCR)** — not elder/APS rules [M06-1][M06-5].
- A late check for an older adult confused about a “helper” managing bills points toward elder exploitation and voluntary APS/financial protections described in **M05 Chapter 6 A/D** — capacity and consent matter [M06-2][M06-6].
- A staff member deliberately leaving a resident in soiled briefs or using a gift card points toward facility neglect and staff misconduct/conduct boundaries described in facility licensure and Justice Center/DOH oversight [M06-3][M06-7] — not a community-voluntary model.

Your job is to notice patterns, ensure immediate safety, consult early, and **route to the right chapter’s process** — not to decide the final legal label. This chapter defines each concept in plain language, then shows how they overlap with M04/M05 and how to cross-reference instead of copying numbers.

2. What Neglect Means — Plain Language, Without Diagnosis
Neglect centers on **unmet essential needs when someone depends on a caregiver or responsible adult or program for those needs**. What counts as “essential” and who is responsible depends on setting, age, licensure, and care plan [M06-1][M06-2][M06-8].

Common forms in everyday words [M06-1][M06-2][M06-5]:
- **Care or supervision neglect:** Inadequate food, clothing, shelter, hygiene, medications, medical appointments, school attendance, or supervision for age/needs; unsafe/unsanitary conditions.
- **Developmental/educational neglect (children):** Persistent lack of support for basic development/education when caregiver can provide or access help [M06-5].
- **Self-neglect (adults 18+ with impairment in community):** Adult’s own difficulty meeting essential needs due to physical/mental impairment — introduced in **M05 §2** [M06-2] — distinguished from facility neglect where staff/agency is responsible.
- **Facility/institutional neglect:** Program or staff failure to provide agreed care (missed meals/meds, inadequate supervision, unsanitary living conditions, failure to seek care for injury/illness) — often a licensure/regulatory matter in addition to any individual report [M06-7].

What neglect is not — without minimizing:
- A single missed meal, one set of unwashed clothes, or a one-time late bill is not alone proof of neglect. Look for **pattern, severity, persistence, and caregiver capacity/access to help** — and always check context [M06-8].
- Poverty alone is not neglect when caregiver seeks or would accept available help but help is insufficient [M06-1][M06-5][M06-11]. Document efforts and offers, not assumptions.
- Choosing a different parenting or living style (food preferences, cultural practices) is not neglect unless it puts essential needs/health/safety at risk [M06-8].

What is not your job: You do not decide “this legally is neglect,” do not assess medical capacity, and do not investigate — you document observable facts and ensure safety, then refer [M06-9].

Devon example: “watched by 16-year-old sibling until midnight, sometimes no food, unwashed clothes, missed school” may suggest supervision/care neglect — pattern, not one evening — and points to **M04 route** for next steps (see §6), not an elder/APS route. Facility example “leave in soiled briefs until shift change” may suggest facility neglect by staff omission — points to facility/DOH/Justice Center route (see §6), not voluntary community APS as primary.

3. What Exploitation Means — Plain Language, Without Diagnosis
Exploitation centers on **using a person’s resources, labor, access, or vulnerability for personal gain** — often through trust, authority, or confusion [M06-2][M06-6][M06-12].

Common forms in everyday words [M06-6][M06-7][M06-12]:
- **Financial exploitation:** Misusing money/property — missing funds, unusual withdrawals, sudden changes in power of attorney or banking, unpaid bills despite adequate income [M06-6]; see M05 §2 financial exploitation details. In workplaces/facilities: staff borrowing or using client funds/gift cards, pressuring signatures, “managing money for free” without agreement [M06-7].
- **Labor or service exploitation:** Forcing or pressuring a person (child or adult with disability) to work or provide services through threat, deception, or misuse of authority [M06-12].
- **Sexual exploitation / trafficking indicators (not detailed):** If you suspect trafficking or sexual exploitation, ensure safety, do not confront, and follow immediate-safety and law-enforcement/facility steps per policy — see §6 and §9 for NYC/NYS human trafficking hotlines [M06-12].

What exploitation is not:
- Accepting a thank-you note is not exploitation; accepting ongoing expensive gifts, loans, or access to bank accounts usually is inappropriate and often prohibited by agency policy [M06-4].
- A family member helping with bills with permission and transparency is not exploitation; a “new friend” with secrecy, control of documents, or pressure is a red flag [M06-6].

Ms. Ortiz example — “new friend manages bills for free, now checks late, bank calls about unusual withdrawals”: possible financial exploitation — secrecy, new helper controlling finances, lack of understanding — warrants supportive conversation with consent/capacity awareness (see §7) and referral for assessment (see §6 M05 A/D), not confrontation of the friend alone.

4. What Inappropriate Conduct and Boundary Violations Mean
Many concerns are not yet “abuse/neglect/exploitation” in legal terms but still require immediate pause, consultation, and internal reporting because power imbalance exists [M06-4][M06-3].

Everyday indicators that call for pause and supervisor consultation [M06-4][M06-8]:
- **Power/role misuse:** Staff as friend, partner, or family — favoritism, special privileges, secrets, one-on-one time outside role, personal communications on private channels.
- **Gifts, money, or favors:** Accepting or giving gifts/money/loans, borrowing client items, side errands for personal gain.
- **Secrecy and isolation:** Encouraging person to hide contact, not tell family/supervisor, or isolating from other staff/services.
- **Physical/emotional boundaries:** Unnecessary touching, comments on appearance, sharing personal problems, threatening or demeaning language, harsh physical handling during care.
- **Documentation gaps:** Doing things off-record, asking not to chart, inconsistent explanations.

Coworker example — “won’t remember — leave in soiled briefs, borrow gift card”: that language names both **facility neglect (omitted care)** and **exploitation/conduct violation (misuse of property + demeaning attitude)** — report through facility misconduct/financial impropriety route and ensure immediate care (see §6), not “wait until shift change.”

Your role: You do not decide if conduct meets a criminal threshold; you ensure dignity/safety, document what you saw/heard in person’s own words, and notify per agency conduct/licensure route [M06-4].

Overlap clarified:
- **Neglect overlaps M04 and M05 and facility:** Child supervision/hunger (Devon) uses M04 lens; elder self-neglect or caregiver failure to provide care (M05 Ms. Ortiz/Mr. Alvarez) uses M05 lens; staff leaving resident soiled uses facility lens [M06-7] — same word “neglect,” different test and route.
- **Exploitation overlaps M05 and M06 facility:** Older adult financial exploitation (Ms. Ortiz) maps to M05 §2/§6 A/D; staff misuse of gift card maps to facility financial impropriety/Justice Center/DOH [M06-7] — financial exploitation is the shared concept, reporting depends on setting.
- **Inappropriate conduct → can become neglect/exploitation/abuse if continued:** Secrecy, gifts, favoritism are early boundaries requiring consultation even before a legal threshold is met [M06-4].

**Do not duplicate reporting instructions here — cross-references control.** If you copy numbers, you risk applying child SCR prior-approval rules to voluntary APS, or APS consent assumptions to child/facility mandatory duties.

6. Prevention — What You Can Do Without Diagnosing or Taking Over Decisions
Prevention centers dignity, adequate care, and proportionate supports [M06-3][M06-8][M06-11]:

- **Adequate care planning:** Ensure care plan matches needs — enough staff, supervision for age/needs, meals/meds on schedule, safe sleep/transfer, medical follow-up — and advocate when plan impossible with current resources [M06-7][M06-8].
- **Reduce isolation and burden:** Friendly visitors, respite for caregivers, adult day, benefits help, concrete supports (food, housing, transportation) — poverty supports vs neglect prevention [M06-11].
- **Money safeguards with permission:** Encourage transparent, documented money management (representative payee with accounting, limited power with safeguards, bank alerts) — only with consent/legal authority [M06-6]. For Ms. Ortiz: offer benefits counseling, bank fraud unit, legal services options; do not take over finances yourself.
- **Boundaries training:** Keep paid caregiving distinct from personal relationships — decline gifts/loans, chart accurately, avoid one-on-one secrecy [M06-4].
- **Supervision adequacy — the “16-year-old babysitter” nuance:** New York does not set a single babysitter age law; adequacy depends on child’s age/needs, duration, sibling maturity, emergency plan, and alternative care options — document concerns as pattern, not one-size threshold [M06-5]. Ask “What would adequate supervision look like for Devon at night given his age/needs?” and route to M04 for next steps, not personal judgment.

If you sense judgment (“they’re just lazy”), pause — offer concrete-help framing: “What supports would make adequate care achievable for this family/person/staffing level?”

7. Consent, Capacity, and Least-Restrictive Protective Interventions — Without One Universal Rule
People have different rights by age, capacity, and setting. Explain interventions without pretending one rule (e.g., “always remove,” “always need permission,” “always APS/SCR”) fits all [M06-10]:

- **Children under 18:** Generally cannot consent to risk/harm; protective steps do not require child or parent consent to report to SCR — see **M04 §5-7** [M06-5]. Do not apply elder “ask permission first” to child safety.
- **Adults 18+ with capacity (including many older adults):** Presumed able to make their own decisions; offered services are typically voluntary — APS assessment often seeks consent, and refusal with capacity is respected [M06-6][M06-10]. For Ms. Ortiz, ask permission to refer/report and explain limits kindly: “I can’t keep this private if facility duty requires report, but I can advocate and help you choose next steps — APS, bank, law enforcement — would that help?”
- **Adults 18+ where capacity is in question:** Do not decide capacity yourself — seek assessment via medical/legal process; in the meantime, prioritize safety, use least-restrictive options (more visits, trusted supported decision-making, benefits protections), and follow facility/APS escalation if eligible under impairment + inability to meet essential needs without willing caregiver [M06-2][M06-10]. Guardianship is court-only, not staff-decided.
- **Licensed facility residents/clients:** Facility has duty to ensure care; protective steps (ensure immediate care, separate staff where required, preserve evidence, plan of correction) operate alongside individual-report routes via DOH/Justice Center — facility cannot rely on “resident refused, so no action” when licensure duty exists [M06-7].

**Least-restrictive in practice:** Prefer options that protect health/safety with fewest limits on rights — e.g., Ms. Ortiz keeps accounts but adds bank alerts + benefits counseling + APS consultation; Devon’s family gets overnight childcare resources + food supports + follow-up, not automatic punishment; resident gets immediate care + independent advocacy (Ombudsman where nursing/adult home) + regulatory follow-up [M06-11].

**Immediate safety first (all settings):** If medical emergency, injury, crime in progress, or imminent serious harm — stay if safe, follow agency emergency plan, **call 911**, then make the setting-matched report/referral without delay and notify supervisor ASAP. If supervisor unavailable, do not delay emergency — same exception as M02 §5B / M04 §7 / M05 §7.

**Then route — see full instructions in those chapters:**

Glossary — Plain Language
**APS voluntary:** Community assessment/services for adults 18+ with impairment unable to meet needs without willing caregiver — typically seeks consent [M06-2][M06-6].
**Capacity:** Adult presumed able to decide unless formally determined otherwise via medical/legal process [M06-10].
**Conduct/boundary violation:** Misuse of power/access — gifts/loans, secrecy, favoritism, off-record, harsh handling — consultable even before legal threshold [M06-4].
**DCJS/DOH/Justice Center:** Official agencies by setting — distinct from SCR/APS [M06-7].
**Exploitation:** Using resources/labor/vulnerability for personal gain — financial, labor, sexual/trafficking [M06-6][M06-12].
**Immediate safety:** First ensure protection (911 if needed) then refer/report — not conditional on supervisor [M06-9].
**Inappropriate conduct:** Boundary-crossing behavior requiring policy review, not yet legal label [M06-4].
**Least-restrictive:** Smallest limit on rights that still protects health/safety [M06-10][M06-11].
**Neglect:** Pattern of unmet essential needs where dependency/caregiver/program duty exists [M06-1][M06-2][M06-7].
**Ombudsman:** Advocate for nursing/adult home residents — distinguishes from enforcement [M06-10].
**SCR statutory vs APS voluntary vs facility mandatory:** Three test families — do not interchange [M06-5][M06-2][M06-7].
