**[Opening 0:00-0:50 — 50 sec]**
> "Everyone deserves to feel safe at work, at home, and in places like stores or buses. Sometimes people experience unwanted sexual comments, messages, or pressure. In New York, the law calls this sexual harassment when it is based on sex, sexual orientation, or gender identity. What counts as illegal is different depending on where it happens. Let's look at what this can look like, and what options you have."

**[Employment definitions 0:50-2:20 — 90 sec]**
> "At work, people talk about two patterns. Quid pro quo means this for that. It is when someone with power says a job benefit, like hours or a promotion, depends on sexual behavior. Hostile environment is when unwelcome sexual conduct makes work conditions worse. Under federal law, this usually has to be severe or pervasive. Under New York State law, it does not. State law says it is unlawful when it subjects you to inferior terms and is more than petty slights. Under New York City law, you need to show you were treated less well because of gender. In New York City, protections against gender-based harassment apply to all employees no matter how big or small the employer is. Training requirements are a separate rule. New York State requires all employers regardless of size to provide annual training. New York City also requires employers with 15 or more employees or one or more domestic workers to provide annual training that meets city standards."

**[Housing federal standard 3:50-5:10 — 80 sec]**
> "Harassment does not only happen at work. In housing, federal law says there are also two forms: quid pro quo, where an unwelcome request is made a condition of rent, repairs, or services, and hostile environment, where conduct is severe or pervasive enough to interfere with use and enjoyment. A single quid pro quo incident can violate the law. Housing providers can be directly responsible for their own conduct, for failing to correct harassment by their employee or agent when they knew or should have known, and for failing to correct harassment by a third party, like another tenant, when they knew or should have known and had power to correct. A provider cannot punish the person who reported, such as by eviction, as a way to fix the problem."

**[Options + privacy + DHR report vs complaint 8:25-9:40 — 75 sec]**
> "If something happens, there is no single right way to respond. Think about safety first. It can help to write down what happened soon — date, time, place, exact words, witnesses, and how it affected you. Save messages. If you want to tell someone inside work, know that supervisors, HR, or designated persons may have a duty to report and may not be able to keep your disclosure completely private. For confidential information before deciding, you can call the New York State free hotline 1-800-HARASS-3. That hotline does not file a complaint for you. When you contact the Division of Human Rights, the information you submit using the online forms or the Call Center is not yet an official complaint. The Division reviews your report and, if appropriate, prepares a formal verified complaint for you to sign. That signed complaint starts the investigation."

**[Retaliation 9:40-10:55 — 75 sec]**
> "Retaliation is punishment for reporting harassment, opposing it, or taking part in an investigation. It is illegal. Examples include cutting hours, changing to less desirable shifts, or threats to evict in housing. City guidance says retaliation includes any action reasonably likely to deter someone from engaging in protected activity."

**[Supporting + 5Ds 10:55-11:35 — 40 sec]**
> "If someone tells you about harassment, listen without interrupting. Thank them for trusting you. Avoid judging. Ask what would be helpful and respect their decisions. Be clear about your own responsibilities. You can say, I'll respect your privacy. Before sharing anything, let's discuss what support you want and any responsibilities I have. For bystanders, the Five D's can help: Direct, Distract, Delegate, Delay, Document. This model started with Green Dot's three D's and was expanded by Right To Be, formerly Hollaback!, which added Delay in 2015 and Document in 2017."

**[Closing resources + deadlines 11:35-14:30 — 175 sec — includes 130 sec cards + 45 sec disclaimer]**
> "In New York State, you can file a discrimination report with the Division of Human Rights. For any act on or after February 15, 2024, the ordinary deadline is three years from the most recent incident. For older acts, contact the Division or an attorney about possible exceptions. In New York City, the Commission on Human Rights generally has a one-year deadline, and three years for gender-based harassment including sexual harassment. For federal employment claims, the EEOC says a charge must be filed within 180 days from the date of the alleged violation, and this deadline is extended to 300 days if there is a state or local anti-discrimination law that covers the location and basis. This 300-day extension is conditional, not universal. The statute says a charge shall be filed within 180 days after the alleged practice, except that in a case where the person has initially instituted proceedings with a State or local agency with authority, such charge shall be filed within 300 days. For federal housing claims, the Fair Housing Act says an aggrieved person may, not later than one year after an alleged practice has occurred or terminated, file a complaint with the Secretary. For court, an aggrieved person may commence a civil action not later than 2 years after the occurrence or termination. The time during which an administrative proceeding was pending is not counted. Always check official agency sites before acting. This video is general information, not legal advice."
