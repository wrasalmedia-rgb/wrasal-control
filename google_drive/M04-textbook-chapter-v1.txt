Opening Situation
Maya, a peer specialist who works with families at the same Bronx wellness center, is co-facilitating a parent support group. One Tuesday, three separate moments happen:

First, 10-year-old Sofia comes in with her older brother Jamal. Sofia has a fading bruise on her cheek and says quietly, “My mom’s boyfriend got mad last night.” Jamal quickly adds, “She fell at the park — she’s fine.” Maya notices Sofia seems withdrawn and avoids eye contact, different from her usual talkative self.

Second, in group, a parent, Mr. Ortiz, says, “Sometimes I leave my kids home alone while I work a night shift — they’re 7 and 9, but my neighbor checks once. Is that okay? I don’t have childcare.” He looks worried, not defensive, and asks what help exists.

Third, after group, a coworker tells Maya, “I think Sofia’s family needs a report, but let’s wait and bring it to team meeting Friday — our supervisor likes to review first. Don’t call the Register yet.” Maya also remembers her training: as a peer specialist she is required to report by agency policy, and some coworkers on the team are statutory reporters listed in law.

Maya wants to protect children, support families without shaming, and follow the right reporting steps — and she worries about getting it wrong, being wrong, or making a family afraid to return. This chapter looks at everyday questions: What might suggest abuse or maltreatment — and what is not your job to decide? How can you help prevent harm while in role? When must you report, what is the difference between the law’s duty and your agency’s policy, and how do you make a report without delaying for permission?

1. Why Recognition, Prevention, and Reporting Matter
Children rely on adults to notice, help, and protect. When concerning patterns are noticed early and families get support — safe supervision options, guidance on discipline, connections to childcare, housing, or mental-health resources — harm can be reduced and families can stay together safely when possible.

Reporting is not punishment. In New York, a report to the Statewide Central Register is a request for the child-protective system to assess safety, not a finding that abuse happened. It starts a process to check on the child and connect the family to services or interventions if needed [M04-1][M04-5]. Recognizing signs early, offering prevention help, and reporting when your role requires it are how frontline staff protect children while respecting families.

Carried forward from M02: Your role matters. Whether you have a personal legal duty to report depends on whether your title is listed as a statutory mandated reporter in law [M04-5] — not only on whether your agency says “everyone reports.” Understanding which rule applies to you prevents delay.

2. What Abuse and Maltreatment Mean — Plain Language, Without Investigation
New York law defines child abuse and maltreatment, but everyday work starts with recognition — noticing patterns that may suggest a child needs help, not deciding legal outcomes.

Common forms described in plain language:
- Physical: injury where explanation does not fit (frequent bruises/burns/fractures, especially in infants, pattern marks), child flinches from contact [M04-1].
- Neglect/lack of supervision: child consistently without adequate food, clothing, shelter, medical care, or supervision for age/development; young children left alone for long periods without appropriate care [M04-1][M04-2].
- Emotional: patterns that harm self-worth or development — constant belittling, terrorizing, isolating, or exposing child to severe domestic violence.
- Sexual: any sexual contact or exploitation of a child.

What is not your job: You do not investigate, interview the child in detail, diagnose, or decide if it legally counts as abuse [M04-5]. Your job is to notice, support the child calmly, ensure immediate safety if needed, and report when your duty applies — let trained investigators ask detailed questions to avoid re-traumatizing or contaminating information [M04-1].

Signs are patterns, not single proof. One bruise alone may be an accident. Repeated injuries with changing explanations, injuries to non-mobile infants, fearful withdrawal, or a child’s disclosure in their own words — together — may suggest the need to report [M04-1]. Age matters: leaving a 15-year-old home alone for an hour is different from leaving a 7 and 9-year-old overnight without appropriate care — follow supervision guidance below [M04-2].

3. Risk Factors and Protective Factors — Why Context Matters
No single factor predicts harm. Risk factors increase stress on families; protective factors buffer stress and support safe parenting. Knowing both helps you offer help without judgment — as in Mr. Ortiz’s night-shift question.

Common risk factors in everyday language (more stress → more need for support):
- Economic stress, unstable housing, food insecurity.
- Lack of safe childcare or supervision options, especially for night shifts or emergencies.
- Social isolation — few trusted adults to call for help.
- Parental mental-health or substance-use challenges without support.
- Intimate partner violence in the home.
- Child with high needs (medical complexity, disability) without adequate supports [M04-3].

Protective factors (things that help keep children safe):
- Nurturing attachment, positive discipline, clear expectations appropriate to age.
- Concrete supports: safe childcare, transportation, benefits help, respite.
- Social connections: family, neighbors, faith, peer groups who can help briefly.
- Parental resilience and knowledge of child development.
- Timely medical and behavioral health care [M04-3][M04-6].

Your role: Connect to supports before crisis when possible — as with Mr. Ortiz — while still meeting your reporting duty if threshold is met.

4. Prevention in Your Role — Safe Supervision, Positive Discipline, Concrete Help
Prevention is not lecturing parents. It is offering practical options that reduce risk, especially around supervision and discipline.

Safe supervision in New York — everyday guidance:
- Young children (generally under 12–13) generally should not be left alone for extended periods; overnight alone is especially concerning for elementary-age children [M04-2]. There is no single age in statute — use maturity, length of time, access to phone/adult, and safety of environment [M04-1][M04-2].
- “Neighbor checks once” during night shift for 7 and 9-year-olds is generally not appropriate supervision for overnight [M04-2] — ask: Is there a responsible adult present or on call who can act immediately? Can the child reach help?
- School-age alone time for short, structured periods with check-ins may be appropriate for older youth if safety factors are in place — your agency can advise.
Your response to Mr. Ortiz: Normalize asking, don’t shame: “Thank you for asking — night work with no childcare is hard. Leaving 7 and 9 overnight with only a once-a-night check generally isn’t considered safe supervision for that age — let’s see what supports exist.” Then connect: emergency childcare, family supports, employer schedule options, after-school programs [M04-6]. Document offer of prevention help.

Positive discipline:
- Alternatives to physical punishment: clear routines, praise, choices, time-in, natural consequences, teaching calm-down skills [M04-3].
- Help parents respond to stress: pause, breathe, call trusted person, safe place for child while caregiver calms. If you see frustration escalating, offer to help find resources, not to judge.

Concrete help within role: housing applications, benefits, food, childcare vouchers, medical scheduling — stay within job description, don’t lend money or provide personal childcare/transport outside policy [M04-7].

If you sense judgment in yourself (“They should know better”), pause — ask what support would have prevented this situation, and bring resources.

5. Who Must Report — Statutory Duty vs Agency/Employer Policy (Carried Forward from M02)
This distinction is the foundation of this module — carried forward verbatim from M02 V1.1/V1.2 so lesson and assessment match:

Statutory mandated reporters — personal legal duty:
People listed in Social Services Law §413(1)(a) — such as physicians, nurses, social workers, teachers, police officers, child care providers, and others in that law’s list — are required by law while acting in their professional capacity to immediately make a report or cause a report to be made to the Statewide Central Register when they have reasonable cause to suspect child abuse or maltreatment [M04-5]. The law states: No school, school official, child care provider, foster care provider, residential care facility provider, hospital, medical institution provider or mental health facility provider shall impose any conditions, including prior approval or prior notification, upon a member of their staff specifically required to report under this title [M04-5 §413(1)(c)]. In everyday words: your employer cannot require you to get permission or to tell a supervisor before you report — do not wait for Friday team meeting or for supervisor review to satisfy the legal timing. After you report or cause a report, you must immediately notify the person in charge after the report was made (if you are the person in charge, ensure the report was made) [M04-5 §413(1)(b)]. If you ask a supervisor to call for you — “cause a report to be made (for example: ensuring that a supervisor makes the report)” per NYC Administration for Children’s Services [M04-5] — you must still ensure the report was actually made, obtain the Call I.D., and ensure you are named if more than one staff had reasonable cause — simply telling a supervisor without ensuring the report was filed does not satisfy the duty [M04-5 NYC ACS]. You need reasonable cause to suspect, not proof [M04-5].

Agency/employer policy reporters — workplace duty only:
Many agencies require all staff (including peer specialists like Maya and Sam) to report by agency policy even when the law does not list them [M04-5]. That creates a workplace reporting responsibility, not a law-based mandated-reporter status — it does not change the law for statutory reporters and does not itself create statutory immunity. If you are reporting only because policy says “all staff report,” follow your agency handbook steps — which may include telling a supervisor first — but that policy cannot be applied to delay a statutory reporter’s immediate legal duty above [M04-5].

Threshold — reasonable cause: You have reason to suspect based on what you saw/heard (child’s words, pattern of injuries, concerning supervision disclosure) while acting in professional capacity — you do not need to prove or witness the abuse [M04-5]. Document facts, not opinions.

What the coworker got wrong in the Opening Situation: Telling Maya to “wait until Friday team meeting — supervisor likes to review first — don’t call yet” would be an impermissible delay if Maya is a statutory reporter with reasonable cause — the law prohibits prior approval as a condition [M04-5]. Even for policy-only reporters, delaying through weekend when safety may be at risk violates prevention/protection purpose — follow agency steps but do not delay appropriate protection.

6. How to Make a Report to the Statewide Central Register (SCR) — What Helps
When threshold is met in your role:

**Immediately make a report or cause a report — do not wait for permission (statutory) or unnecessarily for next meeting (policy).** Use SCR phone or online portal per your agency training:
- SCR hotline for mandated reporters: `1-800-635-1522` (24/7); general public `1-800-342-3720`; NYC ACS guidance cited [M04-5]; TDD for hearing hard: `1-800-638-5163`; online via NYS Mandated Reporter portal where available [M04-1].
- You will be asked for: Call I.D. will be given — write it down; LDSS-2221A written report within 48 hours after oral report where required [M04-5].

What helps the register (bring what you have, do not investigate to get more):
- Child/family identifying information (names, DOB, address/phone, language) as known — not to guess.
- What you observed/heard in the child’s/parent’s own words — quote briefly, avoid interpretation: e.g., “Sofia said ‘Mom’s boyfriend got mad last night’ — cheek bruise fading ~2cm, withdrawn, brother said fell at park.”
- Pattern: prior concerns, frequency, timing of Mr. Ortiz night-shift disclosure (7 and 9 overnight, neighbor checks once) — his statement in his words.
- Immediate safety concerns: Is child safe now? Where? Who is with them? Need for immediate response?
- Your role, where concern observed (professional capacity), and how to reach you for follow-up [M04-1][M04-5].

After you report or cause a report:
- Immediately notify person in charge after report was made (statutory) or follow handbook notification (policy) — include date/time of call, Call I.D., who took report if known [M04-5].
- If you caused a report via supervisor, verify: Did supervisor actually call? What Call I.D.? Are you named? If not, make the report yourself promptly — do not assume it happened [M04-5].
- Document facts (date, time, what was said, observable, who was notified, Call I.D.) without diagnoses or stigmatizing labels [M04-7]; store per agency HIPAA/Part 2 rules [M04-8].

Cultural humility while reporting: Use family’s stated language and names correctly; do not add opinions about culture as cause. Report facts observed.

7. Immediate Safety — Ensuring Protection Without Investigating
If a child may be in immediate danger, do not delay protection for paperwork or supervisor search — prioritize safety while still meeting reporting duty:

- Stay with child/family if safe and within role; do not confront alleged person causing harm in a way that risks safety.
- If danger is immediate (e.g., child left alone now, credible threat tonight, injury needing care) or a supervisor/clinical lead cannot be reached promptly, **do not wait for permission** — ensure immediate safety first: call 911 for medical/law-enforcement emergency, follow agency emergency and elopement plan, then contact supervisor as soon as possible and still make SCR report immediately [M04-4][M04-8] (mirrors M02 emergency exception — “contact supervisor” must not delay emergency response).
- For Mr. Ortiz: He asked for help, not reporting yet unless concerning supervision meets threshold and is ongoing risk. Offer prevention now, assess threshold: 7 and 9 alone overnight with only once-a-night neighbor check — if ongoing and leaves children without appropriate supervision, reasonable cause may exist — report promptly while also offering concrete supports; never shame.
- For Sofia: Fading bruise + “boyfriend got mad” + brother’s explanation that doesn’t fit pattern + withdrawn behavior — may together create reasonable cause while acting in professional capacity — do not interview Sofia in detail; keep her safe, reassure calmly, report promptly per duty, notify after.

You do not need to prove — trained child-protective staff will assess safety at home and coordinate with law enforcement/health as needed [M04-1].

8. What Happens After a Report — And Your Ongoing Role
A report triggers an assessment, not an automatic removal:

- SCR screens report; if accepted, local Department of Social Services/ACS investigates or assesses — home visit, interviews, safety plan, services [M04-1][M04-5].
- Families may receive preventive services, childcare, counseling, medical follow-up, or Family Court involvement if needed [M04-1][M04-6].
- You may be contacted for additional facts — share what you observed, not opinions, within your role and confidentiality rules [M04-8].

Your ongoing role:
- Continue supportive relationship with child/family unless agency directs otherwise; avoid telling family “I reported you” in a way that may endanger child or breach safety plan — follow supervisor/agency guidance on what to say [M04-7].
- Keep documenting services offered, referrals made, family engagement.
- Reflect with supervisor: What did you notice well? What felt hard? What prevention resources would help next family?

Outcome is not yours to control. Your duty was to ensure concern was reported promptly and correctly — whether SCR accepts or not, you met role.

Question: You made SCR report (Call I.D. obtained, supervisor notified after). What is your ongoing role and where to verify current rules?
A) You decide outcome — SCR acceptance means removal.
B) Continue supportive relationship per agency direction (don’t confront “I reported you” in risky way), document ongoing services/referrals, reflect with supervisor; verify current rules at ocfs.ny.gov, nysmandatedreporter.org, nysenate.gov/legislation/laws/SOS/413, nyc.gov/acs [M04-1][M04-5] — SCR screens → local DSS/ACS assessment.
C) Assume you did wrong if child stays home.
D) Ignore family after report.
Correct Answer: B) Ongoing support per agency direction, reflect, verify at official OCFS/NYSenate/ACS sites. Two-option valid (B vs A/C/D collapse).
Objective: Explain what happens after report and where to find official help.

Glossary — Plain Language
**Abuse:** Injury or harm to child where explanation does not fit, or acts that harm wellbeing — includes physical, sexual, emotional patterns.
**Call I.D.:** Number given by SCR when report made — write down, include in documentation and supervisor notification.
**Cause a report:** Asking another person (e.g., supervisor) to call SCR for you — you must still ensure it was actually made and you are named if multiple had cause [NYC ACS] — not simply telling supervisor [M04-5].
**LDSS-2221A:** Writeen report form (paper/electronic) required within 48 hours after oral SCR report where applicable [M04-5].
**Maltreatment/neglect:** Failure to provide adequate food, clothing, shelter, medical care, supervision for age; includes unsafe supervision [M04-1][M04-2].
**Reasonable cause:** Reason to suspect based on what you saw/heard in professional role — not proof [M04-5].
**SCR (Statewide Central Register):** NY central hotline/portal 1-800-635-1522 mandated / 1-800-342-3720 public that screens reports and routes to local child-protective services [M04-1][M04-5].
**Statutory mandated reporter:** Person listed in SSL §413(1)(a) with personal legal duty to immediately report/cause report [M04-5].
**Policy reporter:** Staff required to report by agency/employer policy — workplace duty, not statutory status — follow handbook but cannot delay statutory duty [M04-5].
**Prior approval/notification condition:** Employer requiring permission or notice before statutory report — prohibited by law as condition [§413(1)(c)] [M04-5].

Reviewer Notes — Jargon Outside Learner Text
Statutory list: SSL §413(1)(a) includes physicians, chiropractors, nurses, dentists, optometrists, medical examiners, residents/interns, psychologists, social workers, police, DA/assistants, investigators, child care providers, educators, etc. — check current law via nysenate.gov; list changes [M04-5].
Reasonable cause: threshold “reasonable cause to suspect” while acting in professional capacity — not certainty/proof — see OCFS Pub1159 [M04-5].
413(1)(b) notify after: Immediately notify person in charge after report made (if you are person in charge, ensure filing) — distinct from prohibited prior [M04-5].
413(1)(c) prohibited conditions: No prior approval/notification imposed on statutory staff [M04-5 nysenate.gov Official publisher].
SCR vs CPS: SCR is statewide intake; local DSS/ACS does assessment/investigation [M04-1].
CAPTA: Federal Child Abuse Prevention and Treatment Act — minimum federal definitions, states may be broader [M04-4].
