Opening Situation
Maya works part-time at a neighborhood coffee shop in Queens. She likes her customers and is saving for college. A new shift manager starts making comments about her clothes. He texts her after her shift and asks for photos. When Maya says she is not comfortable, he cuts her hours the next week from 25 to 12. The change affects her pay and her schedule, and it feels like punishment for saying no.
The same week, Maya's cousin Luis, who rents an apartment nearby, tells her that water is leaking from his ceiling. His landlord says he will fix the leak sooner if Luis will go on a date with him. Luis just wants the repair.
This chapter looks at questions like these. It explains what sexual harassment is, how it can look different at work, at home, and in places that serve the public, what rights you have in New York, and what options you have if it happens to you or to someone you know.
Learning Goals — Short, Scannable
After reading this chapter, you will be able to:

1. Why This Topic Matters
Everyone deserves to work, live, and get services without being pressured, humiliated, or punished because of sex, sexual orientation, gender identity, or gender expression. When harassment happens, people often feel unsafe, stressed, or isolated. It can affect health, sleep, pay, school, and housing.
In New York, the law is clear that harassment based on these characteristics is not allowed. Under state law, employers are responsible for providing a workplace free from harassment based on protected characteristics [1]. This applies to all employers, no matter how small, and includes households that employ a nanny or housekeeper. The state also requires every employer to establish a sexual harassment prevention policy and give it to employees, and to provide annual sexual harassment prevention training [1].
New York City has its own protections. City law protects people from gender-based harassment, which includes sexual harassment, at work, in housing, and in public accommodations like stores and restaurants. The city's frequently asked questions explain that protections against gender-based harassment extend to all employees regardless of the size of their employer [2], while the requirement to provide annual sexual harassment training applies to employers with 15 or more employees or one or more domestic workers [2].
2. What Sexual Harassment Means — Different Tests for Different Settings
Sexual harassment is a form of discrimination based on sex. It is illegal, but the exact test depends on where it happens.
At Work
**Federal law:** Unwelcome sexual advances, requests for sexual favors, and other verbal or physical conduct of a sexual nature is sexual harassment when it affects employment or creates an intimidating, hostile, or offensive work environment. In federal court, judges often look for conduct that is severe or pervasive.
**New York State:** State law uses a lower threshold. Harassment is unlawful when it subjects you to inferior terms, conditions, or privileges of employment because you belong to a protected group, such as sex, sexual orientation, or gender identity [8]. Conduct has to be more than petty slights or trivial inconveniences, but does not have to be severe or pervasive.
**New York City:** City law is broader. You show you were treated less well than other people because of gender [2][3]. Protections against gender-based harassment apply to all employees regardless of employer size [2]. Training requirements are separate: state requires all employers regardless of size to provide annual training [1][9]; city requires employers with 15 or more employees or one or more domestic workers to provide training that meets city standards [2].
Unwelcome means not wanted. Focus is on how person receiving conduct experiences it, not just intent. Mutual welcome flirting is not harassment. Harassment can be based on sex, sexual orientation, gender identity, or gender expression.
Two patterns: quid pro quo (this for that) when someone with power links benefit to sexual behavior; hostile environment when unwelcome conduct makes it harder to work, live, or use service. Under state and city employment law, single serious incident can be enough if more than petty slight, and repeated lower-level conduct can add up. Under federal law, courts look more for severe or pervasive.
In Housing — Federal Rule
Federal rule at 24 CFR §100.600 explains quid pro quo and hostile environment harassment because of sex may violate Fair Housing Act [6]. Quid pro quo means unwelcome request where submission is made condition of sale, rental, availability, or terms and conditions of housing or services [6]. Single incident may be enough if it shows quid pro quo or severe hostile environment [6].
Housing provider liability at 24 CFR §100.7: directly liable for own conduct [7]; directly liable for failing to correct employee or agent when knew or should have known [7]; directly liable for failing to correct third party when knew or should have known and had power to correct [7]; prompt action may not include punishing aggrieved person, such as evicting [7]; also responsible for discriminatory practices by agent or employee acting as part of job — vicarious liability in plain language [7].
In Public Accommodations
NYC public accommodations — stores, restaurants, clinics, gyms, theaters, transit — protected against gender-based harassment [2][3]. Business may have responsibility if knows and can act.
3. Recognizing Conduct, Pressure, and Misuse of Power
Examples: sexual comments about appearance, sexual jokes, showing sexual images, unwanted touching, repeated requests for dates after no. Pressure direct or indirect: offering benefit for sexual behavior, threatening negative consequence, pattern of attention wearing you down, control over assignments. Power from schedule, pay, repairs, ability to evict, customers, tips, age, experience. Maya manager used hours; Luis landlord used repairs.
4. Workplace Situations and Responsibilities
Who is Protected
State: all workers regardless of size — employees, interns, contractors, vendors, gig, domestic workers [1]. City: gender-based harassment protections all employees regardless of size [2]; other discrimination generally 4+ employees, but gender-based harassment exception [3]. Freelancers and independent contractors covered for harassment in city and counted for training threshold [2].
Who Can Harass
Supervisor, coworker, third party like client or customer. State says harassment by customers is covered. Manager should try to protect employee.
What Employers Must Do
State: all employers regardless of size must establish policy, give copy, provide annual training [1][9]. City: employers with 15+ employees or 1+ domestic worker must provide annual training meeting city standards [2]. So NYC employers must meet both: state all employers plus city if 15+ or domestic worker. Must post notices. City requires English and Spanish posting and fact sheet [11]. Since 2019, failure to complain to employer is not determinative of employer liability under state law [8].
Everyday Workplace Examples (Full Sentences)
Maya's situation quid pro quo: manager links benefit to sexual behavior then cuts hours. Inferior terms because sex under state [8], treated less well because gender under city [2], protection regardless of size [2], possible retaliation.
Group chat sexual memes after request to stop: repeated unwelcome conduct, inferior terms or less well.
Server harassed by customer: third party covered, manager must try to address.
Remote work comments or images in video meeting covered; state model includes remote workers [9].
5. Housing and Public-Facing Services
Housing
Federal: quid pro quo definition [6], single incident may violate [6], liability direct own conduct [7], no eviction as correction [7]. Luis landlord conditioning repair on date is quid pro quo condition of services [6]. If repair denied after no, affects housing terms. Other examples: maintenance worker advances during repairs, threat of eviction after refusal, tenant-on-tenant provider knows and can correct [7].
Federally assisted housing: Violence Against Women Act may provide emergency transfer.
Deadlines — Official sources:
HUD administrative: An aggrieved person may, not later than one year after an alleged discriminatory housing practice has occurred or terminated, file a complaint with the Secretary [13]. This language comes from 42 U.S.C. §3610.
Federal court: An aggrieved person may commence a civil action in an appropriate United States district court or State court not later than 2 years after the occurrence or the termination of an alleged discriminatory housing practice [14]. The computation of such 2-year period shall not include any time during which an administrative proceeding under this subchapter was pending [14]. This language comes from 42 U.S.C. §3613.
State: DHR 3 years for acts on or after Feb 15, 2024 [4][5]; older acts seek guidance [4].
Public Accommodations
NYC stores, restaurants, clinics, gyms, theaters, transit covered [2][3].
6. Options When Something Happens to You
No single right way. Safety first. If unsafe, move to safe place, reach trusted person. Assault may be crime — police report separate from civil rights complaint.
Write down what happened soon: date, time, place, exact words or actions, witnesses, effect. Save messages. Notes soon after more helpful than memory.
Tell someone inside if you want — understand reporting duties: supervisors, HR, designated persons may have duty to report or investigate and may not keep disclosure completely private [8]. For confidential information before deciding, consider NYS hotline 1-800-HARASS-3, not your employer, does not file complaint.
Learn government options — report vs formal complaint distinction: DHR explains the information submitted using online forms or Call Center is not yet an official complaint, and a discrimination report is not automatically a formal complaint [8]. DHR reviews submission before verified complaint is prepared and filed [8]. You first file a report, DHR reviews jurisdiction and deadlines, then if appropriate prepares formal verified complaint for signature. That signed complaint starts investigation. Choice of where to file affects whether you can file elsewhere later.
Deadlines — without categorical expiration language: DHR official page says for any act on or after Feb 15, 2024, file report within three years of most recent incident [4]. For acts before Feb 15, 2024, timeline depends on type: workplace sexual harassment three years, other types ordinary deadline one year before change [4]. Extension to three years for all claims effective Feb 15, 2024 [5]. For older incidents, contact DHR or qualified attorney about possible exceptions rather than assuming no option [4][5].
Other agencies:
NYC CCHR: one year generally, three years for gender-based harassment including sexual harassment [2][3].
EEOC: Official EEOC timeliness — A charge must be filed with EEOC within 180 days from the date of the alleged violation, in order to protect the charging party's rights. This 180-day filing deadline is extended to 300 days if there is a state or local anti-discrimination law [15]. Youth page: There are strict time limits for filing a job discrimination complaint with the EEOC. In some cases, you only have 180 days to report discrimination to EEOC. You have 300 days if your complaint is also covered by a state or local anti-discrimination law [15]. Statute: A charge under this section shall be filed within one hundred and eighty days after the alleged unlawful employment practice occurred, and notice of the charge shall be served upon the person against whom such charge is made within ten days thereafter, except that in a case of an unlawful employment practice with respect to which the person aggrieved has initially instituted proceedings with a State or local agency with authority to grant or seek relief, such charge shall be filed within three hundred days after the alleged unlawful employment practice occurred [16] — 42 U.S.C. §2000e-5(e)(1) [16].
Take care of yourself: Support from friends, family, a counselor, or community resources can help you cope with stress and make decisions. Reaching out is not overreacting.
7. Supporting Someone and Responding as a Witness
If someone tells you about harassment: listen without interrupting, thank for trusting, avoid judging, let them know you believe them and not their fault. Ask what would be helpful, respect decisions. Be clear about your own role. If supervisor, manager, HR, designated reporting person, you may be required to share information or report and may not promise complete confidentiality [8]. If peer, still should not promise complete privacy; respectful approach: "I'll respect your privacy. Before sharing anything, let's discuss what support you want and any responsibilities I have." Encourage documentation, offer to help find resources, offer to go with them if safe and they want company.
Bystander Five D's: started with Green Dot three D's — Direct, Distract, Delegate — expanded by Right To Be, formerly Hollaback!, added Delay in 2015 and Document in 2017 [10]. Direct: speak up if safe. Distract: create distraction interrupting. Delegate: ask someone with more authority. Delay: check in after. Document: write down date, time, place, exact words or actions, witnesses. Avoid recording video risking privacy; written notes safest. Often delay most supportive.
8. Understanding Retaliation
Retaliation is punishment for reporting harassment, opposing it, or taking part in investigation. Illegal under NYS, NYC, federal law. Protected activities include filing internal or agency complaint, testifying, helping investigation, opposing harassment verbally or informally, reporting another employee harassed, encouraging coworker to report. Retaliation can be hours cut, less desirable shifts, transfer, increased workload, exclusion, threats of eviction in housing. City guidance says retaliation any action reasonably likely to deter protected activity [2][3]. Document what changed, when, who decided. May add retaliation claim. Deadlines tied to retaliatory act itself.

**11. Quid pro quo — Workplace example**
Question: Which is an example of quid pro quo sexual harassment?
A) A coworker tells a single off-color joke that no one reports.
B) A manager says you will get a better schedule if you go on a date with him.
C) A customer compliments the music in the shop.
D) Two coworkers who are dating eat lunch together.

Glossary — Plain Language
**Bystander:** Sees harassment but not directly involved.
**Discrimination report (DHR):** First step submitting information via Call Center or online. The information submitted is not yet an official complaint [8].
**Formal verified complaint (DHR):** After review, if appropriate, DHR prepares formal complaint for signature under oath. Signed complaint starts investigation. Report alone not automatically formal complaint [8][4].
**Gender identity/expression:** Internal sense and outward showing; includes transgender, nonbinary.
**Hostile environment:** Unwelcome conduct that makes it harder to work, live, or use service. Threshold differs.
**Public accommodation:** Places offering goods or services to public.
**Quid pro quo:** Benefit depends on sexual behavior.
**Retaliation:** Punishment for reporting, opposing, participating.
