Opening Situation
Jamal, a community health worker at the Bronx wellness center, works with older neighbors who live at home and with families. One week, three moments happen:

First, 78-year-old Mr. Alvarez, who lives alone and has diabetes and mild memory changes, comes in with his nephew Luis. Mr. Alvarez has a bruise on his forearm and says, “I fell getting the mail — I’m fine.” The home health aide who visits twice weekly previously told Jamal privately, “He seems more confused, and there’s often no food in the kitchen. I offered help, but he said his nephew handles money and he doesn’t want trouble.” Jamal notices Mr. Alvarez is quieter, wears the same clothes as last visit, and Luis answers all money questions for him.

Second, while visiting a partner nursing home for a health fair, Jamal hears a certified nursing assistant whisper, “Mrs. Chen was rough-handled during a transfer and now has a skin tear — we should report, but let’s wait for the evening supervisor.” Jamal knows the nursing home is a licensed facility with its own reporting rules.

Third, 82-year-old Ms. Washington, a former client now in an adult home, tells Jamal on the phone, “My roommate took my debit card ‘to help pay bills’ and won’t give it back. I told the front desk, nothing happened.” She sounds worried about retaliation and asks Jamal not to tell her family.

Jamal wants to protect older adults from harm, respect their right to make decisions, and follow the right reporting steps for each **setting** — and he worries about assuming, overstepping, or reporting to the wrong place. This chapter looks at: What might suggest elder abuse, neglect, or exploitation — and what is not your job to diagnose? What settings matter? Where do you refer or report in New York — and why community elder-abuse rules are not the same as child-abuse (M02/M04) rules?

1. Why Recognition, Prevention, and Reporting Matter for Older Adults
Older adults deserve safety and dignity. When concerning patterns are noticed early and supportive options are offered — home-delivered meals, benefits help, caregiver respite, trusted visitors, benefits counseling — harm can be reduced and independence can be supported.

Elder abuse takes a toll on health, finances, and trust. Yet older adults, unlike children, generally have the **right to make their own decisions** about living arrangements, money, and services if they have capacity [M05-1][M05-5]. Reporting is therefore more complex: some reports are referrals for voluntary assessment and support, not automatic investigations without consent.

Important difference carried forward as contrast: Child-abuse reporting in New York uses a single Statewide Central Register (SCR) with statutory listed reporters who must immediately report without prior approval [M02/M04][M04-5]. **Elder-abuse routes are different and depend on setting** — do not apply child SCR/413 rules to elder situations by analogy. This chapter teaches elder routes afresh.

2. What Elder Abuse, Neglect, and Exploitation Mean — Plain Language, Without Diagnosis
New York uses several terms. Everyday work starts with recognizing patterns that may suggest an older adult needs help, not deciding legal outcomes.

Common forms in plain language [M05-1][M05-2]:
- Physical: injury where explanation does not fit pattern (frequent bruises, burns, fractures, especially with changing explanations; rough handling during care; medication withheld) [M05-1].
- Emotional/psychological: threats, intimidation, isolation from family/friends, belittling that causes fear or withdrawal.
- Neglect — including self-neglect: consistently inadequate food, clothing, shelter, medical care, medications, hygiene, or supervision for needs; unsafe/unsanitary conditions; failure of caregiver to provide needed care [M05-1][M05-2]. Self-neglect is an adult’s own inability to meet basic needs due to impairment [M05-5].
- Financial exploitation: misuse of money/property — missing funds, sudden changes in banking or legal documents, new “helper” controlling finances, unpaid bills despite adequate income, pressure to sign documents [M05-2][M05-6].
- Sexual: non-consensual sexual contact.
- Abandonment: desertion of older adult by responsible caregiver.

What is not your job: You do not diagnose abuse, determine capacity, or decide if it legally counts as abuse [M05-1][M05-5]. Your job is to notice patterns, support the older adult calmly, ensure immediate safety if needed, and **refer or report to the route that matches the setting** — let APS, DOH, Justice Center, or law enforcement assess.

Signs are patterns, not single proof. One fall may be an accident. Combined pattern — Mr. Alvarez’s bruise + different explanations + food insecurity + nephew controlling money + withdrawn, same clothes — may together suggest need for referral, while respecting that Mr. Alvarez may choose to decline some help if he has capacity.

3. Risk and Protective Factors — Why Context Matters
Risk factors increase vulnerability; protective factors buffer stress. Knowing both helps you offer help without judgment — as with Mr. Alvarez’s possible self-neglect plus family involvement.

Common risk factors in everyday language [M05-2][M05-3]:
- Social isolation — lives alone, few visitors, limited transportation.
- Cognitive impairment or dementia, physical frailty, multiple chronic conditions, depression.
- Dependence on others for daily care, money management, or housing.
- Caregiver stress, substance use, mental-health challenges, financial dependence on older adult.
- Financial strain, unstable housing, food insecurity.
- History of family conflict or domestic violence.

Protective factors [M05-3][M05-6]:
- Strong social connections — family, friends, faith, neighbor checks.
- Person-centered care planning that honors preferences.
- Caregiver support: respite, training, support groups, adult day services.
- Concrete supports: home-delivered meals, benefits counseling, transportation, home modifications, money-management assistance.
- Timely medical, behavioral health, and legal planning (advance directives, powers of attorney with safeguards).

Your role: Reduce isolation (“Would a weekly visitor help?”), connect to concrete supports, support caregivers — not to make financial or living decisions for the adult.

4. Prevention in Your Role — Autonomy, Safe Care Planning, Reducing Isolation
Prevention centers autonomy. Support the older adult’s stated preferences and build safety together.

Supporting autonomy and safe care planning:
- Ask what the older adult wants: “What would help you feel safer at home? Who do you trust to help with bills or groceries?” Document stated preferences; do not decide for them if they have capacity [M05-5].
- For Mr. Alvarez: Offer options without taking over — home-delivered meals, benefits review, trusted money-management program, medication organizer, more frequent aide visits — and ask permission before calling family or APS. If he declines, respect choice while reiterating options and safety plan.
- For self-neglect: Offer assessment, not judgment — “I noticed there’s often no food, and you’ve missed medications — what would help?” Coordinate voluntary services via Office for the Aging/APS referral where appropriate [M05-1][M05-4].

Reducing isolation and caregiver stress:
- Encourage regular friendly visitor, senior center, faith group, family care conference where adult consents.
- For family caregivers (like Luis or aides): normalize stress, offer respite, adult day, caregiver support group, training on safe transfers/medication management [M05-3].

Stay within role: Do not manage money, hold legal documents, or provide personal care outside job; do not promise to keep exploitation secret — explain limits kindly early.

If you sense judgment (“He should just leave his nephew”), pause — ask what supports would reduce dependence and increase choice.

5. Why Elder-Abuse Routes Are Not Child-Abuse Routes — Consent, Capacity, and Setting
This is the foundation — researched afresh, not by analogy:

- **No universal SCR equivalent for community elder abuse.** Child abuse uses single Statewide Central Register with statutory list SSL §413 who must immediately report without prior approval then notify after [M02/M04]. Community elder abuse for adults 18+ in New York generally does **not** have that same statutory list imposing immediate report without prior approval via SSL 413. Instead, community concerns for impaired adults are typically referred to **Adult Protective Services (APS) via the local county Department of Social Services** under Social Services Law Article 9-B §473 — which provides voluntary assessment and services for adults with impairments who are unable to meet essential needs [M05-1][M05-5]. Reporting to APS in the community is **generally voluntary** (anyone may refer), not a statutory mandated-reporter duty like child SCR for most community workers [M05-1]. APS often requires the adult’s consent to services; if the adult has capacity and refuses, APS response is limited, unlike child protective where consent not required [M05-5].
- **Capacity and consent matter for elders.** Unlike children, adults are presumed to have capacity to make decisions unless formally determined otherwise; APS seeks consent [M05-5]. Your role: explain options, honor stated choices, ensure safety, document — do not coerce.
- **Setting determines mandated facility routes.** In **licensed facilities and state-operated programs**, different mandatory reporting applies: Nursing homes/adult homes → report to **NY Department of Health (DOH)** and, where applicable, **Justice Center for the Protection of People with Special Needs** for individuals with disabilities in NYS-operated/licensed facilities under their jurisdiction [M05-7][M05-8]; community mental health / substance use / developmental disability programs → report to **Justice Center / OMH / OASAS / OPWDD** per facility rules [M05-7][M05-8] — facility staff often have agency-specific mandatory reporting duties, distinct from child SCR 413. Check facility policy — mandatory reporters are defined by that setting, not by child law.
- **Law enforcement vs APS:** APS is social-service assessment, not law enforcement; if crime is suspected (theft, assault, abandonment) or danger immediate, consider **law enforcement (911)** in addition to APS/facility report per agency plan [M05-4].
- **Do not analogize:** Do not apply child SCR 1-800 numbers, Call I.D. concept, LDSS-2221A 48-hour rule, or §413(1)(c) prior-approval prohibition to elder APS referrals. APS intake, consent, and service model are different. Where facility mandatory reporting exists, follow that facility’s supervisor-notification rule — but as defined for that facility, not as transferred child rule.

What Jamal’s coworker got wrong if analogized: “Don’t call SCR — wait for Friday like child case” mixes routes. Correct: For Mr. Alvarez (community), consider APS referral via local DSS with his input, respecting autonomy; no SCR exists for this adult. For Mrs. Chen in nursing home, facility reporter follows nursing home mandatory report to DOH (+ Justice Center if disability jurisdiction) per facility policy — not child SCR — and should not delay for evening supervisor if immediate care need exists.

6. Where to Refer or Report in New York — By Setting (Everyday Steps)

**A) Community-dwelling older adult (adults 18+ with impairment, including Mr. Alvarez) — APS via local DSS [M05-1][M05-4]:**
- Find APS: contact county Department of Social Services — APS unit; statewide navigation via **NY State Office for the Aging (NYSOFA) / NY Connects 1-800-342-9871** or county DSS directory [M05-4].
- You may call APS to discuss concern and make referral; provide what you observed (bruise, statements in own words, food insecurity, money control, confusion) — not diagnosis [M05-1].
- Where possible, involve the older adult: ask permission to refer, explain APS is voluntary help (assessment, benefits, meals, home care, money-management options) — adults with capacity may decline [M05-5].
- After referral, document facts (date/time/observable/quotes/who notified) [M05-9]; follow confidentiality per HIPAA/agency rules — share minimum necessary.

**B) Nursing home / adult care facility residents — licensed facility routes with distinct DOH contacts per official DOH guidance [M05-2][M05-7a][M05-7b]:**
- Nursing homes (e.g., Mrs. Chen rough handling/skin tear): Facility staff with mandated duties must report per facility policy — to **NYS DOH Nursing Home Complaint Hotline 1-888-201-4563** for nursing homes [M05-7a]; document and preserve evidence; ensure care need addressed immediately. [Source: official DOH Nursing Home Profile via profiles.health.ny.gov — listed separately from adult care.]
- Adult care facilities / assisted living, including adult homes (e.g., Ms. Washington): For adult homes / assisted living, report per facility policy — to **NYS DOH Adult Care Facility Complaint Hotline 1-866-893-6772** for adult care facilities [M05-7b]; document and preserve evidence. [Source: official DOH Adult Care profile via profiles.health.ny.gov — 1-866-893-6772 listed separately from nursing-home hotline.]
- If resident has qualifying disability in a Justice Center jurisdiction (e.g., certain ICFs, state-operated homes), also report to **Justice Center 1-855-373-2122** or via facility Justice Center protocol [M05-8]; Check facility — DOH (nursing vs adult-care number) vs Justice Center vs both depends on licensure and setting.
- Do not wait for next shift supervisor to decide; follow facility urgent reporting timeframe (often immediate oral + written within 24h per policy) [M05-7a][M05-7b].

**C) Mental-health / substance-use / developmental-disability licensed program (OMH/OASAS/OPWDD residential) [M05-7][M05-8]:**
- Staff report via **Justice Center** where jurisdiction applies, otherwise via agency-specific incident reporting (OMH, OASAS, OPWDD incident management). Ask supervisor for “Are we a Justice Center reportable program? What is our incident number?” Provide observable + reporter role [M05-7][M05-8].

**D) Immediate danger or crime regardless of setting (e.g., Ms. Washington debit card theft, assault, abandonment) [M05-4][M05-6]:**
- Ensure immediate safety, stay if safe, **call 911 if medical emergency, crime in progress, or imminent danger**; financial exploitation with ongoing access may also need law enforcement report; APS/facility report still needed separately for safeguarding/advocacy — 911 does not replace APS/facility report and APS does not replace 911.
- For financial exploitation in community: also consider APS referral plus bank/financial institution fraud unit and law enforcement; offer Office for the Aging HIICAP/benefits counseling, legal services [M05-6].

**E) Long-Term Care Ombudsman — resident advocate regardless of reporter [M05-10]:**
- For nursing home/adult home concerns, you or resident may also contact **Long-Term Care Ombudsman** via NYSOFA/NY Connects — advocate who can visit, mediate, inform about rights [M05-10].

After refer/report: notify person in charge per **facility or APS-contact protocol** (community APS is voluntary referral, not statutory “notify after” like child 413(1)(b) — follow your agency handbook for who to tell after referral) [M05-1]; verify referral received (APS reference number where given), not Call I.D. SCR concept.

Cultural humility: Use older adult’s stated language/names correctly; do not add opinions about age, culture, or disability as cause. Work with interpreter if needed [M05-9].

7. Immediate Safety — Ensuring Protection Without Diagnosing
If older adult may be in immediate danger, prioritize safety while respecting autonomy:

- Stay with adult if safe and within role; do not confront suspected abuser in risky way.
- If danger immediate (e.g., assault now, no heat/meds, fall with injury, exploitation with ongoing access) or supervisor cannot be reached promptly, **do not delay emergency response** — ensure immediate safety (call 911 for medical/law-enforcement, follow agency emergency plan), then make APS/facility referral without delay and contact supervisor ASAP [M05-4][M05-9] (same emergency exception principle as M02/M04, applied here to elder routes).
- For Ms. Washington debit card taken, fear retaliation: Ensure safety, offer private conversation, ask permission to help report to facility administration + law enforcement/APS as appropriate, explain retaliation protections via Ombudsman/Justice Center advocacy [M05-7][M05-10]; do not promise secrecy if safety/facility duty requires report — explain limits.
- For self-neglect: If Mr. Alvarez refuses APS, respect autonomy if he has capacity, offer safety planning (who to call, medical follow-up, food options), coordinate with willing family where adult consents, re-offer later [M05-5].

You do not need to prove — APS, DOH, Justice Center, law enforcement assess.

8. What Happens After an APS Referral or Facility Report — And Your Ongoing Role
Referral triggers assessment/support, not automatic removal or arrest:

- **APS (community):** APS worker contacts adult (often with consent), assesses safety, eligibility under SSL Article 9-B §473 (adult with physical/mental impairment unable to protect essential needs), offers voluntary services — home care, meals, benefits, money-management, housing, guardianship referral if lacks capacity and at risk [M05-1][M05-5]. If adult with capacity refuses, APS may close with re-offer. No court removal like child protective without legal process.
- **Facility DOH/Justice Center:** Facility investigates, ensures care, reports to survey/health, may involve law enforcement, may result in plan of correction, staffing changes, or Family/Criminal Court [M05-7][M05-8].
- You may be contacted for additional facts — share observable, not opinions, within confidentiality [M05-9].

Your ongoing role:
- Continue supportive relationship unless agency directs otherwise; avoid telling alleged person “I reported you” in way that endangers older adult — follow supervisor/agency guidance.
- Keep documenting offers, referrals, adult’s stated choices, follow-up services.
- Reflect with supervisor: What signs did you notice? What supports helped? What prevention for next older neighbor?

**52. Risk and protective factors — everyday language**

Glossary — Plain Language
**APS (Adult Protective Services):** Local DSS program under SSL Art 9-B §473 that assesses impaired adults 18+ unable to meet essential needs and offers voluntary services [M05-1]. Not SCR.
**Capacity/consent:** Adult presumed able to make decisions unless formally determined otherwise; APS seeks consent for services [M05-5].
**DOH:** NY Department of Health — licenses nursing homes and adult care facilities; takes complaints via **separate hotlines: Nursing Home Complaint 1-888-201-4563 [M05-7a] vs Adult Care Facility Complaint 1-866-893-6772 [M05-7b]** — see profiles.health.ny.gov.
**Elder abuse:** Harm to older adult — physical, emotional, neglect/self-neglect, financial exploitation, sexual, abandonment [M05-1][M05-2].
**Exploitation:** Misuse of older adult’s money/property [M05-2][M05-6].
**Justice Center:** NY Justice Center for Protection of People with Special Needs — reports abuse/neglect of people with disabilities in state-licensed programs 1-855-373-2122 [M05-8].
**Long-Term Care Ombudsman:** Advocate for nursing home/adult home residents via NYSOFA/NY Connects [M05-10].
**NYSenate.gov:** Official NYS Legislature publisher for SSL — nysenate.gov/legislation — use for law text; Cornell LII unofficial reference [M05-16].
**Ombudsman ≠ APS:** Advocate vs protective services — distinct pathways.
**Self-neglect:** Adult’s own inability to meet basic needs due to impairment [M05-5].
**Statutory vs APS voluntary:** Child SCR statutory [M02/M04] vs community elder APS voluntary referral — not interchangeable [M05-5].

Reviewer Notes — Jargon Outside Learner Text
APS eligibility: SSL Art 9-B §473 — adult 18+ with physical/mental impairment unable to manage essential needs, no willing caregiver — voluntary services; differs from child SCR threshold reasonable cause [M05-1].
Capacity presumption: Adults have right to refuse unless lack capacity determined via legal/medical process — honor autonomy while ensuring safety offer [M05-5].
Facility jurisdiction: DOH for nursing/adult homes; Justice Center for state-operated/licensed disability/mental-health facilities — check licensure; some programs report to OMH/OASAS/OPWDD incident system where Justice Center not exclusive [M05-8].
Mandatory vs voluntary: Facility staff often have mandatory facility-reporting duty (DOH/Justice Center) per regulation/policy — distinct from community voluntary APS referral — do not blend with child 413 list [M05-7].
Financial exploitation: APS + law enforcement + bank; consider Guardianship only through court if lacks capacity [M05-6].
Elder Justice Act: Federal coordination — not NYS APS [M05-4].
