Opening Situation
Luis now works part-time at the front desk of the same neighborhood wellness center in Queens where Sam works. Luis loves greeting people, but he is still learning the desk routine. On Monday morning, three moments happen close together:

First, a new participant, Mx. Rivera, checks in for a peer-training workshop. Their intake form, which Marisol filled out themself, lists name: “Marisol Rivera, pronouns: they/them, language: Spanish — prefers interpreter.” In the “what helps me participate” box Marisol wrote: “Please use they/them and call interpreter — I want to follow along in Spanish and ask questions myself.” Marisol is a community health worker who often advocates for neighbors. Luis, trying to be quick, says “Mari-sol? She is here for 10 a.m.?” and gestures to a seat without offering language help.

Second, an older adult, Mr. Jackson, 62, who is Deaf and uses American Sign Language, arrives without an appointment and writes on a notepad in his own words: “I need help with housing form. I can read English. I prefer ASL interpreter for detailed forms — I can write for quick scheduling.” Mr. Jackson is a retired housing advocate who has been to the center before and knows which forms matter. A coworker whispers to Luis, “We don't have ASL today — can you just write back quickly and get him to come back tomorrow?”

Third, a teenage peer volunteer, Aisha, 17, who wears a hijab and helps plan youth meals, is waiting nearby while a staff member complains loudly in the break area within earshot: “I don't get why we have to change the food options for her.” Last month Aisha asked the kitchen team — in her own words — “Can we list what’s in each dish so everyone can choose? I can help label them.”

Luis wants to be kind, respectful, and efficient — and also worries about saying the wrong thing, taking too long, or making someone feel like a burden. This chapter looks at everyday questions like these: What does cultural competency mean in plain language? How do you show respect for names, pronouns, language, disability, and beliefs while staying in your role? What does inclusion look like at a front desk, in a waiting room, and in the words you choose?

Learning Goals — Short, Scannable
After reading this chapter, you will be able to:
Explain what cultural competency and cultural humility mean in everyday language.
Describe how bias can show up in small moments — and what you can do to reduce it.
Show respect for names, pronouns, and identity in the way you greet and document.
Explain language access: when to offer an interpreter or translated material, and how to work with one.
Give everyday examples of inclusion for disability and accessibility (mobility, hearing, vision) without making assumptions.
Explain boundaries that protect inclusion — what you can do, what your agency must provide, and what needs a supervisor.
Support someone who experienced disrespect or exclusion while staying in your role and being clear about limits.

1. Why Cultural Competency, Inclusion and Respectful Communication Matter
People come for help with their whole lives — language, family, faith, disability, gender, age, race, ethnicity, income, housing. When people feel seen, respected, and understood, they are more likely to trust you, share important information, and keep coming back. When they feel misnamed, rushed, judged, or excluded, they may leave, not ask for help, or feel unsafe — even if you meant to be kind.

Inclusion is not extra — it is part of doing the job well. Simple actions — using the name and pronouns someone gave, offering language help without them having to beg, making space for a cane or wheelchair, explaining what will happen next — build trust and safety for everyone.

Federal guidance from the HHS Office of Minority Health describes the National Standards for Culturally and Linguistically Appropriate Services (CLAS) as a way to help health and human-service organizations provide effective, equitable, understandable, and respectful care that responds to cultural health beliefs, preferred languages, and communication needs [M03-1]. New York City and New York State human-rights resources similarly explain that protections cover race, ethnicity, national origin, disability, gender identity and expression, and other characteristics in employment, housing, and public accommodations like stores, clinics, and transit [M03-2][M03-3]. You do not need to memorize every standard — know where to find your agency’s plan for language, disability, and nondiscrimination, and who to ask when you are not sure.

2. What Culture, Identity, and Inclusion Mean — Everyday Language
Culture is not one thing about one group. It includes shared language, customs, faith, family roles, food, art, history, and everyday ways of doing things — plus the individual ways each person lives them. Identity is how a person understands themselves — including racial or ethnic identity, language, gender identity and expression, disability, age, veteran status, faith or no faith, and more. People often have several identities at once.

Cultural competency in everyday language means you have the knowledge, skills, and respectful attitudes to work well with people from many backgrounds, and your agency has policies, training, and resources to support you [M03-1]. Cultural humility is the everyday practice inside competency: stay curious, admit you don’t know, ask respectfully, listen more than you assume, and let the person be the expert on their own life [M03-4].

Inclusion means everyone can take part with dignity — physical access (doors, seating, kiosk height), communication access (interpreters, captioning, plain-language forms), and belonging (being greeted by the name and pronouns you use, seeing your community reflected, not being singled out) [M03-5][M03-6].

New York example: City law protects people from discrimination based on actual or perceived race, ethnicity, national origin, disability, gender identity or expression, and other protected characteristics in public accommodations [M03-2]. State resources explain how to report and where to seek help for discrimination statewide [M03-3]. These protections exist alongside federal civil-rights rules like Title VI (language access for programs receiving federal funds) and the Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA) for disability access [M03-6][M03-7].

3. Bias in Everyday Work — Recognizing Small Moments and Repairing
Bias is a tendency to favor some people or ideas — often without realizing it. Implicit bias is a quick, automatic association that can affect how you listen, who you believe, how long you wait, or what you assume someone needs. It does not mean you are a bad person — it means your brain took a shortcut that you can notice and change.

Small moments where bias can show up:
Greeting one person with a smile and another with a sigh.
Assuming a teenager with a hijab wants special food rather than asking the group what options help everyone feel welcome.
Assuming an older Deaf adult cannot read English or cannot fill out a form without checking what they prefer.
Mispronouncing a name and not correcting yourself, or correcting someone else’s pronouns only when they are not in the room.

What helps:
Pause and notice: “What am I assuming before I ask?”
Ask the person directly, respectfully: “How would you like me to address you? Would an interpreter be helpful? What is the best way for us to communicate today?”
Correct yourself kindly and move on: “Thank you for telling me — they/them — I’ll make that change. What you said about needing extra time at the kiosk — let’s make space for that.”
Document respectfully: Use the name, pronouns, and language the person provided on their form; avoid adding opinions like “difficult” or “demanding” [M03-7][M03-8].
Learn team patterns: If one group often waits longer, misses appointments, or leaves without service, bring it to a supervisor as a possible system issue, not a person’s fault [M03-1][M03-7].

Repair matters more than perfection. A brief, sincere correction builds more trust than pretending it did not happen.

4. Respectful Communication — Names, Pronouns, and Identity (distinct from language access)

Names: Use the name the person gave on the form or when introduced. If you are not sure how to say it, ask: “Did I say your name correctly? I want to say it right.” Repeat it back once you learn it. Avoid shortening without permission or making jokes about names. If you mispronounce, correct quickly: “I’m sorry — Marisol, thank you for correcting me.” [M03-2][M03-8] This is respectful communication about identity — it is about how you address someone, not about which language they need.

Pronouns: Pronouns are words like she/her, he/him, they/them that people use for themselves. New York City human-rights guidance (NYC Commission on Human Rights Gender Guidance) explains that gender identity and expression, including pronouns, are protected characteristics, and that using pronouns consistent with a person’s self-identification is part of respectful treatment in public accommodations and workplaces [M03-2]. Everyday practice: use the pronouns the person provided on the intake form they filled out themself, mirror what the person uses for themselves, and if you slip, correct briefly without long explanations that center your feelings: “They said they will be here at two — thank you for reminding me.” Do not ask intrusive questions about bodies, medical history, or “real” names [M03-2][M03-8]. Ask once, then use consistently: “Thank you for sharing they/them — I’ll note that so you don’t have to repeat it. How would you like to be addressed today?”

What respectful communication is not: It is not guessing gender from appearance, and it is not inferring pronouns from clothing, voice, or name — ask what the person uses, then use it [M03-2].

4B. Language Access — A Separate Obligation with Separate Sources (Title VI)

Language access: Under Title VI of the Civil Rights Act of 1964 for programs receiving federal financial assistance, you generally must take reasonable steps to provide meaningful access for people with limited English proficiency — often through qualified interpreters or translated materials, not by requiring family members to interpret [M03-6]. Federal guidance at LEP.gov explains how to plan for interpretation and translation [M03-6]. Even where Title VI does not apply, many New York agencies provide language help by policy because it is effective and equitable [M03-1][M03-6]. This is distinct from names/pronouns: language access is about the right to understand and be understood in your preferred language.

Working with an interpreter — ask, don’t infer:
Offer early: “An interpreter can join us at no cost — would you like one in Spanish?” [M03-6] Do not infer language need from surname, accent, or appearance — ask what the person prefers and document what they tell you [M03-6].
Use a qualified interpreter (in-person, video, or phone) rather than a child, friend, or the front-desk staff who “speaks a little Spanish” for complex or sensitive topics [M03-6][M03-8].
Speak to the person, not the interpreter: “What brings you in today?” rather than “Ask her what brings her in.” Pause for interpretation.
Document language preference the person stated so the next staff does not have to ask again [M03-7].

Luis’s first moment missed these steps: using “She” instead of the they/them Marisol wrote themself, mispronouncing “Marisol,” and not offering the Spanish interpreter Marisol requested in their own words. An in-role repair that asks rather than assumes could be: “Good morning, Mx. Rivera — thank you for your form. You wrote you use they/them and prefer Spanish with an interpreter so you can ask questions yourself — thank you for telling us. I’ll make sure we use the right name and pronouns on your paperwork, and I can call Video Interpreter now — is that what you’d like today?”

5. Inclusive Practices — Disability, Accessibility, Belonging Without Assumptions (distinct legal basis: ADA)
Disability includes many lived experiences — mobility (cane, wheelchair, chronic pain), sensory (Deaf, hard-of-hearing, low vision, blind), cognitive, mental health, and chronic illness. The Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA) requires equitable access for people with disabilities in public programs and public accommodations, which often means reasonable modifications, auxiliary aids and services, and accessible facilities [M03-7]. This is a disability-accommodation obligation, separate from language access under Title VI — different law, different aids, different sources (ADA.gov [M03-7] vs LEP.gov [M03-6]).

Everyday inclusive practices — ask what the person wants, don’t infer from appearance:

Mobility — ask, don’t assume need from cane: Do not move someone’s cane, bag, or wheelchair without permission. Offer extra time and space at kiosks, counters, or waiting areas — but ask first: “What is most helpful for you at the kiosk today? Would you like extra time or a different way to complete this form, or are you all set?” If a line is long, offer a seat or a pager/number so they can wait comfortably. Do not infer from a cane that someone cannot stand briefly or cannot use a touchscreen — ask [M03-7]. At the center, Ms. Thompson, 68, who uses a cane after knee surgery, told the front desk herself: “I like to take my time at the kiosk — can I have an extra minute? I don’t need someone to fill it for me.” That is agency — she stated preference, staff followed it.

Hearing — ask preference, don’t infer reading ability or signing: Do not assume reading ability from Deaf identity, and do not assume everyone signs. Mr. Jackson, in his own handwritten note, wrote “I can read English. I prefer ASL interpreter for detailed forms — I can write for quick scheduling.” That is individual preference — follow it: offer options — qualified ASL interpreter (in-person or Video Remote Interpreting), captioned phone, written exchange for simple topics, or real-time captioning for meetings — and ask which he wants today. “You wrote you prefer ASL for detailed forms and can write for scheduling — we can call Video ASL now, or we can write for scheduling — what works best for you today? Coming back tomorrow is an option, but we have same-day interpreter access if you can wait a few minutes.” [M03-5][M03-7] Many agencies must provide effective communication through appropriate aids and services — “just write back quickly” is often not effective for complex housing forms [M03-7]. Do not infer need from age or appearance — ask.

Vision: Offer verbal wayfinding (“The kiosk is 10 steps ahead on your right”), large-print or electronic forms that work with screen readers, and reading assistance if wanted. Do not touch someone or their guide dog without asking. Ask: “Would you like directions, large print, or to use the accessible kiosk setting?” [M03-7]

Belonging — not singling out: Reflect community in what people see — multilingual signs, diverse images, halal/vegetarian/kosher/vegetarian options noted neutrally, not as “special for her.” When a staff complaint singles out a participant (“change food options for her”), it harms belonging even when the participant overhears it indirectly. Aisha herself had already offered — in her own words — “Can we list what’s in each dish so everyone can choose? I can help label them.” That is agency — the fix was already participant-led; staff should follow it, not debate it. Address complaints privately with a supervisor: “In the break area at 10:15, I heard a comment about food options that referenced a participant’s hijab within earshot of others — I’m concerned it made visitors feel unwelcome,” and model inclusive language: “We offer several food options and list ingredients so everyone can choose comfortably — let’s keep the break area respectful.” [M03-1][M03-8]

Ask, don’t assume — core practice for this section: “What assistance would be most helpful for you today?” is better than guessing needs from one characteristic like cane, hijab, accent, or age — let the person name preference, then provide it [M03-7].

6. Boundaries and Cultural Humility — Staying in Role While Being Respectful
Being culturally humble does not mean you must be an expert on every culture, faith, or disability community. It means you are willing to learn, to be corrected, and to keep the focus on the person’s needs — not your curiosity or comfort.

Helpful boundaries:
Time and role: You provide information, support, and connections within your job. You do not provide counseling outside your training, diagnose, or make decisions for someone. If a question is outside your role, say so and connect to the right person: “I’m not able to change your benefits, but our benefits specialist is here until 4 and has an Amharic interpreter available — may I introduce you?”
Questions: Ask only what your agency needs to provide service. Avoid “Where are you really from?” or “What’s wrong with you?” Instead: “How can I best support you during this visit?”
Self-awareness: Notice when you feel uncomfortable, impatient, or overly helpful, and pause. Discomfort often signals a chance to learn, not a reason to avoid someone [M03-4].
Privacy: Do not share someone’s identity, language, disability, or faith with other participants as an example.

Cultural humility in action: If you misnamed or misgendered someone, correct it for yourself and note it respectfully in the file (per agency rules) so the next person does not repeat it — not to label the person, but to avoid asking again. Thank the person for correction: “Thank you for telling me your pronouns are they/them — I’ll update the form.”

7. When Respect and Safety Intersect — Responding to Disrespect or Harm
Respect does not mean ignoring harm. If you hear demeaning language — about food, clothing, accent, disability, or identity — you have a role to reduce harm and keep the space safe.

If you overhear a staff complaint that targets a participant:
Stay calm. Do not repeat the language loudly or confront harshly in front of the participant.
Support the person who may have been affected: acknowledge quietly if they seemed to hear it, offer a private space, and ask what would help now: “I’m sorry if that was uncomfortable to hear. We want everyone to feel welcome here. Would you like to speak with a supervisor or move to a quieter waiting area?”
Address with a supervisor promptly: name what you heard, where, and what impact you noticed, without diagnosing intent: “In the break area at 10:15, I heard a comment about food options that referenced a participant’s hijab within earshot of others. I’m concerned it made visitors feel unwelcome.” [M03-7][M03-8]
Document facts (date, time, words or gist, who was affected, what you did) per agency process.

If bias affects service (e.g., someone is told to “come back tomorrow” only when others are helped today), flag it as a possible access issue, not just a one-person delay: “We don’t have ASL in person, but we have Video Remote Interpreting — I offered that and note we should log language/communication aid requests.” [M03-6][M03-7]

If someone reports harassment or discrimination in your program, follow your agency’s nondiscrimination and complaint steps and connect them to official resources (City Commission on Human Rights, State Division of Human Rights) rather than deciding the legal outcome yourself [M03-2][M03-3].

8. Building Trust Across Differences — Small Actions That Add Up
Trust grows from many small, consistent moments:
Greet each person by the name and pronouns they provided — consistently, not only when they correct you.
Offer language help proactively when form says “prefers interpreter” — don’t wait to be asked.
Make accessibility routine: keep paths clear, offer extra time, keep interpreter video equipment ready, test captioning.
Explain what happens next: “After check-in, you’ll meet with a counselor for about 20 minutes. I can stay with you until they are ready, or you can wait here — what would you prefer?”
Follow up as promised: If you said you would update the file, tell them it is done — “I added that you prefer Spanish with interpreter and they/them, so you won’t have to repeat it.”

Accountability: If you learn you repeated a name or pronoun error in notes, correct the file per agency rules and tell the person the correction is made. If a process keeps disadvantaging one community (longer waits, missed language help), bring data patterns to a supervisor — inclusion is a team and system responsibility [M03-1][M03-7].

**31. Cultural competency vs cultural humility**
Question: In everyday language, what is the difference between cultural competency and cultural humility?
A) Competency is knowledge/skills/attitudes and agency resources to work well across differences [M03-1]; humility is the ongoing practice of curiosity, admitting what you don’t know, asking respectfully, listening, letting the person be expert on their life [M03-4].
B) They mean the same thing — just different words.
C) Humility means you must know every culture completely.
D) Competency means you no longer need to ask questions.
Correct Answer: A) Competency is the broader organizational/interpersonal capacity — knowledge, skills, attitudes, policies, resources [M03-1] taught in §2 and practiced when Luis corrects his greeting; humility is the ongoing interpersonal stance [M03-4] — staying curious, admitting gaps, asking respectfully, letting Marisol be expert on their own pronouns/language — practiced Example 1. Why others wrong: B collapses distinct concepts; C mistakes humility for expertise; D mistakes competency for not needing questions — competency requires ongoing asking [M03-1][M03-4].
Objective: Explain cultural competency and cultural humility in everyday language. Teaching §2, practice Example 1, assessment KC31 with explanatory answer.

**32. Bias in a small moment — what to do**
Question: Luis notices he greeted one participant warmly and another briefly, and wonders if bias affected it. What is the most helpful next step?

Glossary — Plain Language
**Belonging:** Feeling included and valued, not singled out, in a space.
**Bias:** Tendency to favor some people/ideas — often automatic — that can affect service.
**Cultural competency:** Knowledge, skills, attitudes + agency supports to work well across cultures [M03-1].
**Cultural humility:** Everyday practice of curiosity, admitting gaps, asking respectfully, letting person be expert [M03-4].
**Effective communication (ADA):** Communication with people with disabilities that is as effective as with others, via appropriate aids/services [M03-7].
**Inclusion:** Physical, communication, belonging access so everyone can take part with dignity.
**Language access (Title VI):** Steps to provide meaningful access for limited English proficiency, often via qualified interpreters/translated materials [M03-6].
**Protected characteristic:** Race, ethnicity, national origin, disability, gender identity/expression, religion, etc., protected by human-rights law [M03-2][M03-3].
**Reasonable modification:** Change in usual way of doing things to provide access, unless it fundamentally alters nature of service [M03-7].

Reviewer Notes — Jargon Outside Learner Text
CLAS Standards: HHS National Standards for Culturally and Linguistically Appropriate Services — 15 action steps across Governance/Communication/Engagement — principal standard: provide effective equitable understandable respectful care responsive to cultural health beliefs, preferred languages, health literacy [M03-1].
Title VI + LEP gov: Title VI Civil Rights Act 1964 §601 prohibits national-origin discrimination including language access for federally funded programs; LEP.gov provides federal language-access planning guidance [M03-6].
ADA effective communication: ADA Titles II/III require appropriate auxiliary aids/services (ASL, captioning, accessible forms) for effective communication — “just write a note” often insufficient for complex forms [M03-7].
NYC Gender Guidance: NYC Commission on Human Rights Gender Guidance explains gender identity/expression protections including pronoun use in public accommodations/employment [M03-2].
Georgetown NCCC: National Center for Cultural Competence at Georgetown University — research/university reference for cultural competency models — not a government primary source [M03-4].
Belonging vs diversity: Inclusion focuses on experience of being welcomed and able to participate, not mere presence [M03-8].
